Desk stretches you can do without leaving your chair

Ten stretches, 30 to 65 seconds each. Grouped by where it hurts, so you can go straight to the bit that aches.

You do not need a mat, a change of clothes, or for anyone to notice. Most of what follows can be done in a chair, at a desk, in an open-plan office, without drawing attention.

This is the thing people usually get wrong about desk stretching, sometimes called deskercise: they imagine it needs a proper session. It does not. What matters is that it happens often, and the easiest way to make it happen often is to make it small enough to be unremarkable.

If your neck and shoulders ache

The most common complaint, and usually the result of a screen slightly too low and a head slightly too far forward.

45 seconds

Neck side stretch

Sit tall. Drop your right ear towards your right shoulder without rotating your head. Keep the left shoulder down rather than letting it creep up. Swap sides halfway.

45 seconds

Upper trap stretch

As above, but turn your chin slightly towards your armpit as you tilt. This reaches the band running from neck to shoulder-top, which is usually the one that is actually sore.

30 seconds

Shoulder rolls

Slow, full backward circles. Up, back, down, as large as you can make them. The one you can do mid-meeting without anyone minding.

If your upper back feels locked

45 seconds

Chest opener

Clasp your hands behind your back, straighten the arms and lift them gently. Let the chest rise and the shoulder blades draw together. Keep breathing.

45 seconds

Seated spinal twist

Right hand to the outside of your left thigh, rotate the torso to the left. Turn from the ribs rather than hauling on your knee. Swap sides halfway.

65 seconds

Doorway chest stretch

Forearm flat on a door frame, elbow around shoulder height, step forward on the same-side foot. If you feel it in the front of the shoulder joint rather than across the chest, lower the elbow.

If it is your wrists

30 seconds

Wrist circles

Extend both arms, make slow circles in one direction, then the other. Then spread the fingers wide and release, a few times. Thirty seconds, no fuss.

If your hips and legs are stiff

These need you to stand, which is rather the point.

65 seconds

Hip flexor stretch

One foot well forward, front knee bent, back leg straight. Tuck the pelvis slightly and you should feel the front of the back hip lengthen. Stay upright. Swap sides halfway.

45 seconds

Standing quad stretch

Hold the desk for balance, draw one heel towards your backside, keep the knees close together. Swap sides halfway.

45 seconds

Standing hamstring stretch

One heel on the floor in front of you, toes up, hinge forward from the hips with a flat back. You are aiming for the back of the thigh, not your lower back. Swap sides halfway.

The 60-second version. No time? Shoulder rolls, neck side stretch, and stand up for the hip flexor stretch. That is a minute, it covers the three places desk work hits hardest, and it beats a perfect ten-minute routine you never actually do.

How often, and why that matters more than how long

Every 30 to 60 minutes is a reasonable target. Not because stretching needs that frequency, but because sitting is the thing you are interrupting.

That is where the evidence actually points. A 2024 trial found that short bouts of squats taken regularly lowered post-meal blood glucose compared with sitting undisturbed [1]. A separate analysis of wearable data found that very small amounts of vigorous incidental activity were associated with markedly lower mortality among people doing no formal exercise [2].

Those studies are about movement, not stretching specifically. Stretching makes the interruption comfortable and more likely to stick; standing up is what does the work.

If stiffness is mostly about how you are sitting rather than how long, the posture exercises guide tackles that from the other direction.

When to stop

A stretch should feel like a stretch, not a sharp or electric sensation. Pain that shoots down an arm or leg, numbness, tingling, or anything that is steadily worsening is a reason to see a physiotherapist or doctor rather than to push further.
