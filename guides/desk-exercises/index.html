Desk exercises that actually do something

Strength and cardio you can do at work in under a minute, grouped by what you want out of it.

Stretching makes sitting more comfortable. Exercise changes what sitting does to you. They are not the same thing, and if you only have a minute a few times a day, this is the one worth spending it on.

Everything below takes between 30 and 75 seconds, needs no equipment beyond a chair or a desk, and can be done in work clothes.

Start with squats

If you do one thing from this page, make it this one. It is the most researched desk exercise there is, and the findings are unusually specific.

30 seconds

Bodyweight squats

Feet about shoulder-width, push your hips back as though reaching for a chair behind you, keep your chest up, stand back up. Ten of them takes about thirty seconds.

A 2024 trial found that squat breaks taken regularly through the day reduced post-meal blood glucose compared with sitting uninterrupted [1]. The same work reported higher muscle protein synthesis than uninterrupted sitting. That is a lot of return on thirty seconds.

The honest framing. None of this replaces structured exercise. What it does is interrupt the hours of stillness in between, and the evidence suggests that interruption matters on its own terms.

If you want strength

40 seconds

Desk push-ups

Hands on the desk edge, walk your feet back until your body is a straight line, lower your chest towards the desk and press back up. Check the desk is solid before trusting it.

40 seconds

Chair dips

Hands on the front edge of a stable, non-wheeled chair, slide your hips forward off the seat, bend the elbows to lower, press back up. Keep the shoulders down away from your ears.

75 seconds

Isometric wall squat

Back flat to the wall, slide down until your thighs are somewhere near parallel, hold. No movement, no noise, and it is harder than it sounds by about the forty-second mark.

30 seconds

Calf raises

Stand, rise onto the balls of your feet, lower under control. Hold the desk if your balance needs it.

If you want your heart rate up

60 seconds

Marching in place

Lift the knees deliberately rather than shuffling. A minute of this is enough to change how awake you feel.

60 seconds

High knees, slow

As above but higher and more controlled, driving each knee towards hip height. Slow is the point: it is about range, not speed.

45 seconds

Standing side steps

Step out to one side and back, then the other. Sideways movement is almost entirely absent from a desk day.

The ones nobody will notice

Open-plan offices are the real reason people stop doing this. These three are effectively invisible.

60 seconds

Gluteal contractions

Squeeze, hold five seconds, release, repeat. Entirely undetectable, and the muscle in question has been doing nothing for hours.

60 seconds

Soleus push-ups

Seated, feet flat, lift your heels as high as they go while the balls of your feet stay down, then lower. The soleus is the deep calf muscle, and it is one of the few that can keep working for long periods without tiring.

60 seconds

Seated leg extensions

Straighten one leg out in front, hold briefly, lower without letting the foot touch down. Alternate.

How often

Something every 30 to 60 minutes of sitting. Not a routine, not a block in your calendar — one or two of these, a minute, several times a day.

Frequency is doing the work here. An analysis of wearable data found that very small amounts of vigorous incidental activity through an ordinary day were associated with substantially lower all-cause mortality in people who did no structured exercise at all [2]. Movement breaks have also been associated with increased cerebral blood flow and better working memory shortly after standing up [3].

For the stiffness rather than the metabolic side, see the desk stretches guide, or the posture exercises guide if sitting has left you rounded forward.

A sensible caution

Build up rather than starting with the hardest thing on the page. If you have a cardiac condition, a recent injury, or you are pregnant, check with a clinician about what is appropriate. Sharp pain is a stop signal, not something to work through.
