Posture exercises for people who sit all day

Eight movements, 30 to 65 seconds each. No equipment, no floor work, nothing you can't do beside a desk.

Most posture advice tells you to sit up straight. That is not a plan, because the problem is not how you sit for one minute. It is sitting in one position for eight hours.

Hold any posture long enough and your body adapts to it. The muscles at the front of your chest and hips shorten. The ones across your upper back and backside stop doing much at all. Sitting up straight for thirty seconds does not undo that, which is why reminders to correct your posture rarely stick.

What does change things is moving often. The exercises below target the four patterns that long sitting creates, and they are grouped that way rather than as a random list of stretches.

What sitting actually does

Four things, roughly, and each has an obvious counter-movement:

  • Your chest tightens and your shoulders roll forward, because reaching for a keyboard holds them there.
  • Your head drifts forward, which loads the muscles along the back of your neck and the tops of your shoulders.
  • Your hip flexors shorten, because sitting holds them at their shortest length all day.
  • Your glutes go quiet. You are sitting on them and not asking them to do anything.

For rounded shoulders

45 seconds

Chest opener

Stand, clasp your hands behind your back, straighten your arms and lift them gently away from you. Let your chest rise and your shoulder blades draw together. Breathe normally rather than holding your breath.

65 seconds

Doorway chest stretch

Forearm flat against a door frame, elbow at roughly shoulder height. Step forward with the same-side foot until you feel a stretch across the front of your chest. Swap sides halfway. If you feel it in the front of your shoulder joint rather than your chest, lower your elbow.

For a forward head position

45 seconds

Neck side stretch

Sit or stand tall. Drop your right ear towards your right shoulder without turning your head. Rest your right hand lightly on the opposite side of your head if you want a little more. Keep the opposite shoulder down. Swap sides halfway.

45 seconds

Upper trap stretch

Similar start, but turn your chin slightly towards your armpit as you tilt. This catches the band of muscle running from your neck to the top of your shoulder, which is usually where desk tension sits.

30 seconds

Shoulder rolls

Roll both shoulders backwards in a slow, full circle. Up, back, down. Make the circles as large as you can. This one is useful precisely because it is easy enough to do without leaving a meeting.

For tight hips

65 seconds

Hip flexor stretch

Step one foot well forward, bend the front knee and let the back leg straighten behind you. Tuck your pelvis slightly under and you should feel the front of the back hip lengthen. Keep your torso upright. Swap sides halfway.

45 seconds

Seated spinal twist

Sitting, place your right hand on the outside of your left thigh and rotate your torso to the left, turning from the ribs rather than yanking with the arm. Swap sides halfway.

For dormant glutes

60 seconds

Gluteal contractions

Squeeze your glutes hard, hold for five seconds, release. Repeat. It looks like nothing and can be done sitting in a meeting, which is the point: the muscle needs reminding it exists.

Frequency beats duration. A few of these every hour will do more for how you feel at 5pm than twenty minutes of stretching once a day. The research on breaking up sitting points the same way: short, frequent interruptions are what shift the numbers.

How often

Aim for something every 30 to 60 minutes of sitting. It does not need to be all eight. Two movements, ninety seconds, several times a day is a realistic pattern that people actually keep up.

The evidence for frequent interruption is stronger than most people assume. A 2024 trial found that brief bouts of squats taken regularly through the day reduced post-meal blood glucose compared with uninterrupted sitting [1]. Separately, an analysis of accelerometer data found that very small amounts of vigorous incidental activity in daily life were associated with substantially lower all-cause mortality in people who did no structured exercise [2].

Neither study is about posture specifically. They are about the cost of sitting still, which is the thing underneath the posture problem.

If the issue is more stiffness than posture, the desk stretches guide approaches it from the other side.

What posture exercises will not do

They will not permanently reshape how you stand, and anyone promising that is overselling. What they do is reduce the stiffness and aching that comes from holding one position for hours, and keep the muscles involved in an upright posture doing something during the day.

If you have pain that is sharp, persistent, radiating down an arm or leg, or getting worse, that is a reason to see a physiotherapist or doctor rather than to stretch harder.
